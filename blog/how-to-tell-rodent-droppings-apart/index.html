Mouse, Rat or Packrat Droppings: How to Tell Them Apart.

Droppings are often the first physical evidence of a rodent. Size and place help narrow down the animal, and safe handling matters.

Droppings are one of the first things people find, and they are useful. Size, shape, number and place all carry information. They are not a diagnosis, but they help decide where to look.

Size and shape, in general terms.

The measures below are general guidance, and individual droppings vary. A technician confirms by looking at the location, the gnawing and other signs.

Animal.General size and shape.Where often found.
House mouse.Small, about the size of a grain of rice, pointed ends.Drawers, cabinets, behind appliances, garage shelves.
Rat.Larger, thicker pellets.Attics, rooflines, along walls and travel routes.
Packrat.Larger, often with debris or a nest nearby.Near stick and debris nests, vehicles, outbuildings.

Do not rely on size alone. A cluster in an unusual place may belong to a different animal.

Place and pattern matter.

Mice leave droppings scattered widely as they travel and feed. Rats leave fewer, larger droppings in regular spots. Packrats often leave droppings near their nest or along regular paths.

  • Scattered small pellets in a kitchen point to mice.
  • Larger pellets in an attic point to rats.
  • Droppings near a pile of sticks suggest a packrat.
  • Droppings with food scraps or chewed packaging suggest a feeding spot.

Fresh or old.

Fresh droppings are dark and may look slightly moist. Old droppings turn gray and dry out. If you clean an area and new droppings appear the next day, the activity is current.

That test is useful but only if you clean safely. Wet cleanup with disinfectant is the method. Our guide on safe cleanup of rodent droppings lays out the steps.

Do not handle them dry.

Rodent droppings can carry germs, and dry droppings can crumble into dust. The CDC advises against sweeping or vacuuming them. The safer way is to ventilate, wear gloves, wet the droppings and wipe them up, then bag the waste.

If you have developed a fever, aches or trouble breathing after contact with rodent waste, tell your doctor. We are not medical professionals.

What to tell a technician.

  • Where you found droppings and how many.
  • Their approximate size, compared with a coin or a grain of rice.
  • Whether they look fresh or old.
  • Whether there are other signs: gnawing, rub marks, odor.

A photo with a coin for scale is helpful. Leave droppings in place until the visit if you can.

What droppings cannot tell you.

Droppings do not tell you how many animals there are, how long they have been there or how they are getting in. A few droppings can mean one animal passing through, and a pile can mean a nest nearby.

They also cannot tell you whether the animals are still present. The best plan is to combine the evidence: droppings, gnawing, rub marks, noise and smell. Together they give a technician what is needed to name the animal and find the route.

  • Look for gnawing next to the droppings.
  • Look for rub marks on the path between a gap and the food source.
  • Listen at night for activity.
  • Notice any smell, which can mean a nest or a dead animal.

One more caution.

Do not assume the absence of droppings means no problem. Animals can be active in a wall or attic without leaving visible droppings. Noise, smell and gnawing count too.

Before you call: safe steps for rodent signs

Public health agencies give consistent advice on this. The steps below follow the CDC pages linked at the end of this guide.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Before entering a closed space with signs of rodents, open it up and leave for about 30 minutes.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves, and wash your hands well afterward, even if you wore gloves.
  • Dampen droppings and urine stains with disinfectant or diluted bleach, leave it for about five minutes, then wipe with paper towels.
  • Dispose of wipes and droppings in a sealed plastic bag, and keep skin off dead animals.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Sources and standards

Frequently asked

Can I tell the species for certain from droppings?

Not for certain. Size and location are good clues, and a technician confirms with other evidence.

How can I tell fresh droppings from old?

Fresh droppings are dark and moist, while old ones are gray, dry and crumble easily.

Are droppings harmful?

They can carry germs. Do not touch them bare-handed and do not sweep or vacuum them dry.

Should I leave them for the technician?

If you can, yes. Take a photo and leave them in place so the technician can see them.

Do bats or other animals leave similar droppings?

Some can look alike. That is one reason an inspection is worth having.
