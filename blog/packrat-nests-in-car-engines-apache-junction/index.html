Packrat Nests in Car Engines: What to Do in Apache Junction.

A pile of sticks under the hood is a common surprise. Here is what it usually means, what is safe to do and what should wait for a professional.

If you lift the hood and find a heap of sticks, dried plant pieces and other debris, you are probably looking at a rodent nest. In the desert Southwest, a common builder is the packrat, also called a woodrat. This guide explains what to do next.

Do this first.

  • Do not start the engine if you can see nest material or smell something burning.
  • Take photos of the nest and of any chewed wires or hoses.
  • Keep children and pets away from the vehicle.
  • Wear gloves and do not touch droppings or nesting material with bare hands.
  • Call a professional who can handle both the animal and the parking spot.

A vehicle that has been sitting is the most likely home. A car that is driven daily is disturbed too often for most animals to settle in, though they may still visit at night.

Why an engine bay appeals.

To a small animal, an engine bay offers shelter, warmth after a drive and a lot of nooks. The space beside the engine, the air filter box and the area near the battery are all dark and enclosed. Cabin air intakes near the windshield can be reached from outside too.

Packrats are known for collecting material and building a large nest, called a midden, from sticks, cactus pieces and whatever else they find. Mice and rats are also possible, and they build with finer, shredded materials like paper, insulation and fabric.

What the damage looks like.

Rodents gnaw constantly. In a vehicle that can mean wire insulation, hoses, sound-proofing and even plastic fittings. Damage may show up as a warning light, a rough start or an odd smell, or it may show up only when a mechanic looks.

Exposed wire is a safety concern. It can short and, near flammable debris, cause a fire. A mechanic or auto electrician should repair vehicle wiring, and a rodent service should deal with the animal.

Cleaning the nest safely.

Nests and droppings can carry germs. The CDC advises against sweeping or vacuuming them dry. Instead, air out the space, wear gloves, wet the material with disinfectant, wipe it up with paper towels and bag the waste. The steps are in the safety box on this page and the linked CDC guidance.

Stopping a repeat.

Removing the nest is only half the job. If the vehicle goes back to the same spot, the animal can come back too. Look at where you park.

  • Is there food nearby, such as pet food, birdseed or spilled seed?
  • Are there plants, brush or stacked material that give shelter against the parking spot?
  • Does the garage have gaps at the door corners or along the walls?
  • Does the vehicle sit unused for days or weeks?

A technician can check the spot and close the gaps. See garage and basement rodent control for the garage side of this.

Habits that help.

If you can, park in a closed, sealed garage. Remove food and clutter from the garage. Open the hood once a week and look. A small amount of disturbance is often enough to make a vehicle less attractive.

If the vehicle will sit for a long time, check it before you start it. Our guide on leaving a house or vehicle empty has a short list for that.

When to call.

Call if you find a nest, see chewed wiring, smell something burning or musty from the vents, or hear scratching. Call early. A nest that is found on day one is easier to deal with than one that has had weeks to grow.

Safe handling checklist for droppings and nests

Nothing here is specific to our service. It is general cleanup safety, summarized from CDC guidance.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Hand protection matters. Wear gloves throughout and wash your hands with soap afterward.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Symptoms such as fever, body aches or trouble breathing after contact with rodent waste call for medical advice. We cannot assess anyone's health.

Common questions

Can a packrat nest cause a fire?

It can contribute to one. Dry debris sits near hot parts, and chewed wires can short. Do not drive a vehicle with a nest or burning smell until it is checked.

Should I start the car to see if it runs?

Not if you have found nest material or smell something burning. Starting it can overheat debris or run a damaged wire.

Will a mechanic remove the nest?

Some will. A rodent service also checks where the animal came from and how to stop it returning.

Do mothballs or sprays work?

They are not a reliable deterrent and can be hazardous to pets and children. Closing access and removing food and shelter is more reliable.

Can this happen in a garage?

Yes. A closed garage with gaps at the door or walls can still let animals reach a vehicle.
