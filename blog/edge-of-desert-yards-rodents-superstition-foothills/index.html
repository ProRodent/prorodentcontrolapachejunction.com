Edge-of-Desert Yards and Rodents Near the Superstition Mountains.

Apache Junction sits against the Superstition Mountains. If your lot touches open ground, the edge of the property deserves a closer look.

Apache Junction is bounded on the east by the Superstition Mountains, which are federal wilderness. That makes it different from a city surrounded by other cities. Some properties sit right at the edge, and the ground beyond the wall or fence is desert.

Why the lot edge matters.

Open ground is where wild animals live, and it is where many rodents live before they ever find a house. Whether animals cross your lot depends on the lot, not on a general rule. What we can say is that a lot edge is a place to look, and an edge with brush against the wall is more inviting than a clear one.

Lost Dutchman State Park, the Superstition Mountain Museum, Goldfield Ghost Town and Silly Mountain Park are the places most people name for this part of the area. They are all reminders that open desert is close. If your property is nearby, the lot edge may be worth mentioning on the call.

A walk along the lot line.

  • Look for burrows beside walls, slabs, fence posts and under landscaping.
  • Look for droppings and runways along the fence or wall.
  • Check brush, debris and stacked material against the house.
  • Check where the fence or wall meets the house.
  • Check lighting wires, irrigation tubing and pool equipment for gnawing.

Do not poke into burrows or disturb nests. Photograph what you see and leave it.

Between the lot and the house.

If animals cross the lot, the house is the next stop. Gaps at the roofline, garage and utility entries then matter. A seal that is fine in a quiet suburb may be tested by an animal that arrives every night.

Be careful with other wildlife.

Desert land has animals other than rodents. Do not handle animals, and do not reach into burrows or under debris. If you are unsure what an animal is, keep a distance and call.

What to tell the technician.

  • Where your lot meets open ground.
  • What is on the edge: brush, wall, fence, shed.
  • Where you saw burrows, droppings or gnawing.
  • Whether pets roam the yard.

The outdoor visit looks at those points. Backyard and outdoor rodent control describes what is covered.

Sorting the yard into zones.

A simple way to plan is to think of the yard in three zones. The first is the lot edge, where open ground meets your property. The second is the middle of the yard, with plantings, a shed or a pool. The third is the strip against the house itself.

  • Edge zone: keep brush thin, check for burrows and watch for gnawed lines.
  • Middle zone: store feed and fruit carefully and keep stacked material off the ground.
  • House zone: keep a clear strip along the walls and close every gap you can find.

Most of the value comes from the third zone, because that is where an animal either finds a way in or does not.

A seasonal habit.

Walk the lot line each season. A short look in spring and fall can catch changes early. If something looks new, take a picture and call.

Cleanup safety, drawn from CDC guidance

The CDC publishes cleanup steps for homes with rodent droppings or nests. This list restates the main points in plain words.

  • Do not use a vacuum on rodent waste, even a strong one. Wet it down first, as the steps below describe.
  • Ventilate a shut-up space first. Half an hour of airing out is the CDC's starting point.
  • Wear rubber or plastic gloves for the whole job, and scrub your hands with soap and water at the end.
  • Wet first, wipe second. Disinfectant needs about five minutes on the surface before you remove anything.
  • Collect waste and paper towels into a bag, seal it, and discard it with the household trash.
  • Watch your health for several weeks after a cleanup. If symptoms like fever or breathlessness appear, tell your doctor about the rodent exposure.

Things people ask next

Does living next to open desert mean more rodents?

It may, but it depends on the lot. Open ground is a place animals travel from, and the inspection checks whether yours is affected.

Are the Superstition Mountains part of the city?

They bound the city on the east and are federal wilderness. Properties near that boundary may share a lot line with open ground.

Should I clear all the brush?

Clear brush against the house and walls. Clearing a wide area has other considerations, so ask before doing large work.

Do fences help?

A fence does not stop rodents. It can serve as a travel route, so check the fence line.

What should I tell the technician?

Where the lot meets open ground, what is on the edge and where you have seen activity or burrows.
