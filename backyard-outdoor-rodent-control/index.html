Start outside the walls.

A house does not become a target on its own. Rodents arrive because the yard offers food, water and cover, and then they find the gap. Yard work reduces the number of animals that reach the walls in the first place.

The inspection outside covers the full property line. It looks for burrows, droppings, runways and anything that gives rodents a reason to stay.

What the outdoor walk-through covers.

  • Burrows beside slabs, walls, sheds and under landscaping.
  • Brush, debris, stacked wood and unused materials against the house.
  • Palms, citrus, hedges or vines that touch the roof or the wall.
  • Pet bowls, bird feeders, compost and trash cans that animals can reach.
  • Drip irrigation tubing, outdoor lighting wires and pool equipment pads.

If your lot borders open desert, the technician will also ask about the edge of the property. Open ground is a place animals travel from, and a lot line with brush against it makes the trip easy. If your property is near open land such as the areas around Lost Dutchman State Park or Silly Mountain Park, say so when you call and the walk-through will include the lot line.

None of these is dramatic on its own. Together they decide whether the yard feeds a rodent population or starves one.

Burrows, traps and stations.

Where burrows are active, traps and covered stations are placed near the entrances. Burrows are not simply filled in, because that can send animals toward the house. The technician explains the plan and the follow-up checks.

Rodenticide rules are strict. Federal regulations limit consumer products to bait stations, and the EPA page in our sources explains why. In yards with pets, children or wildlife, we prefer traps.

Linking yard work to sealing.

Yard measures reduce the pressure, but they do not close the building. If animals are already in the house, pair this with rodent exclusion and trapping. If the problem is mainly large climbing rats, rat control covers the roofline.

When to do the work.

Yard changes help at any time of year. In the hottest months animals look for water and shade, and in the monsoon months heavy rain can flood burrows and send animals looking for shelter. A seasonal walk-around is a good habit.

Before you sweep, spray or bag anything

Short, practical safety points based on CDC cleanup guidance. They are not medical advice.

  • Avoid any dry cleanup of droppings or nests, because disturbed waste turns into airborne dust.
  • Start by opening doors and windows, then step away for 30 minutes so the air can turn over.
  • Protect your hands with gloves, then wash up carefully even if the gloves look clean.
  • Dampen droppings and urine stains with disinfectant or diluted bleach, leave it for about five minutes, then wipe with paper towels.
  • Collect waste and paper towels into a bag, seal it, and discard it with the household trash.
  • Watch your health for several weeks after a cleanup. If symptoms like fever or breathlessness appear, tell your doctor about the rodent exposure.

Before we arrive

  • Open side gates and tell the technician about any dog that is loose in the yard.
  • Do not trim palms or climbing vines yourself in the middle of an active problem without a plan.
  • Keep a flashlight handy and know where the electrical panel is, in case wiring is damaged.
  • Photograph any droppings, burrows or nests before moving anything.

Yard questions

Do I need yard work if the problem is in the house?

Often yes. If food and cover outside stay the same, animals will keep arriving at the walls.

Do fruit trees attract rodents?

Fallen fruit can. Picking up fruit and keeping fronds and branches off the roof reduces the draw.

What about bird feeders?

Spilled seed feeds rats and mice. Feeders away from the house and a clean ground under them help.

Can I fill burrows myself?

Filling a burrow does not solve it, and you may trap an animal inside. Ask the technician first.

Does a pool or irrigation matter?

Water sources attract animals in a dry climate, and drip lines can be gnawed. Tell the technician where water is available.
